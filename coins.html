For grown-ups

Compound interest means the money you earn starts earning too. In year one, interest is paid on what you saved. In year two it's paid on your savings plus last year's interest, and so on. Growth builds on growth, so the curve looks flat for years and then bends sharply upward.

Rule of 72: divide 72 by the yearly rate to get roughly how many years it takes money to double. 7% a year ≈ every 10 years; 4% ≈ every 18; 10% ≈ every 7.

7% a year is a rough long-run average for the stock market after inflation. Real returns bounce around a lot from year to year, some years are negative, and nothing is guaranteed. 4% is roughly a good savings rate. “Magic 1% a day” is pretend; no real investment does that. It's there to show how fast compounding can run away.

Money coming in: each row adds money on its own schedule, at the end of each day, week, month or year. Paychecks take your yearly salary × the share you save, paid as 12 monthly paychecks; the salary goes up by the raise % every year. Amounts are in today's dollars if you use an after-inflation growth rate like 7%.

The 4% rule: a common retirement rule of thumb says you can spend about 4% of your savings in the first year (then adjust for inflation) and the money has historically lasted about 30 years. So $1,000,000 ≈ $40,000 a year. It's a rough guide, not a promise.

How the math works here: each period's interest is spread evenly across its days, so the treasure grows smoothly and matches “compound once per period” at every period boundary. Results match the standard future-value formulas: lump sum, an ordinary annuity for each row, and a growing annuity for paychecks.

The lesson for kids: start early, add a little often, and leave it alone to grow. This is a teaching toy, not financial advice.
